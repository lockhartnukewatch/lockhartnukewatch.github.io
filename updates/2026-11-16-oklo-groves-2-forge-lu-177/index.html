Groves-2 Forge: a Lu-177 pilot — but where, and under what review?

On October 13, 2026, Oklo announced that it is advancing Groves-2 Forge, an “isotope production and processing pilot facility” that will combine reactor irradiation with hot-cell processing — isotope separation, product recovery, packaging and transportation — under one roof, with an initial focus on lutetium-177 (Lu-177), a radioisotope used in targeted cancer therapies such as Pluvicto and Lutathera.

The announcement came on the back of the DOE’s Nuclear Energy Launch Pad selection (August 24, 2026), following Groves-1’s initial criticality on August 5, 2026, and its clearance for unrestricted operations on November 11 — what that clearance means for the existing site, including the first citable description of Groves-1 in the DOE’s own environmental document, is covered in our companion piece on Groves-1. A DiscoveryAlert article explains the motivation for Lu-177 processing and the difference in the DOE “demonstration” vs NRC “commercial” tracks. We also dug further into articles from American Nuclear Society, Nuclear News roundup from this week, coverage of the October 13 announcement, plus World Nuclear News’ report on the November 11 clearance. All of them fill in several of the gaps below.

“Groves showed how quickly a new reactor could be built and brought to criticality through close collaboration between the private sector and DOE. Groves-2 Forge will build directly on that foundation by adding the capabilities needed to irradiate target materials and process and prepare valuable isotopes.” — Jacob DeWitte, Oklo cofounder and CEO, to Nuclear News

Where will Groves-2 Forge actually be?

None of announcements say. They describe the facility, the program, and the isotope — but not a location. And it’s not just silent; on the November 6 Q3 earnings call, Oklo management said the Groves-2 facility “site is still being finalized,” and listed “starting Groves-2 construction” as a future target “dependent on execution and required approvals.”

In its Fall 2026 Groves Teach-in deck, Oklo describes its deployment playbook as “local permits and community engagement as step one” — language that strongly suggests no site is locked. That matters because the Launch Pad program — launched in March 2026 as the successor to DOE’s 2025 Reactor Pilot Program, which NRIC has now filled with two rounds of selections (four companies, then eight) — has two pathways: one at Idaho National Laboratory, and “Launch Pad USA,” which extends the framework to non-federal sites anywhere in the country.

So on the current record, Groves-2 Forge could be:

  • an expansion of the existing 47-acre Oklo lease at Proto-Town in Caldwell County,
  • a brand new site in Texas or elsewhere, or
  • a facility at INL in Idaho wearing a “Groves” name for marketing continuity.

And here’s the pressure point: the same Q3 company update, as reported by Nuclear News, says Oklo plans to begin construction of Groves-2 Forge within the next 12 months. That is a construction timeline attached to an unannounced site. The company also told Nuclear News it intends to move Groves-2 from pilot operations to commercial scale “with the appropriate [NRC] licenses for commercial operation” — without a word on what that transition actually involves. If the company is serious about that 12-month window, the site question is urgent: the public may learn where Groves-2 Forge is from a TDLR TABS filing rather than from any announcement.

Some trade press (e.g. The Inference) already calls it a “Texas pilot plant.” Is this wild speculation, or a hint of what’s coming to ’town?

If a second reactor — plus a chemical processing plant — is coming to the same backyard in Lockhart, residents should know before the ground breaks, not after. Texas construction is publicly registered with the state; a new TDLR TABS entry for the Proto-Town site would be the tell — the site’s only current registration, the Groves-1 building, is broken down in our companion piece. We’ll be watching.

What’s in the open record about Groves-2

1. There is no DOE environmental review for Groves-2 yet. The DOE NEPA determination for Groves-1 (CX-271014) covers Groves-1 only — a categorical exclusion (B5.26) issued for fuel possession and operation of that one reactor. A production pilot with its own reactor, irradiation positions, and a hot cell will need its own DOE authorization and environmental review. Groves-1’s was published on energy.gov; Groves-2’s should appear the same way. Until that document exists, no one, including Oklo’s press office, can tell you what the safety basis of Groves-2 actually is. (How modest the Groves-1 document is — 100 watts, a hundred hours, “the reactor won’t be refueled” — and how the site’s construction record complicates it, are detailed in our companion piece.)

2. NRC status. Searches of NRC ADAMS for the Groves reactor still yield nothing, for the same reason as before: the pilot-phase reactor runs under DOE authorization, not NRC licensing. The only NRC action so far is the March 2026 materials license for the Idaho radiochemistry laboratory — authorizing isotope handling in Idaho, not Texas. Commercial-scale operation of Groves-2 would eventually require NRC licenses, but Oklo has given no timeline and no docket. One development that makes that bridge more plausible: in October, the NRC finalized a rule — as World Nuclear News reports — allowing license applicants to use analyses, test data, and other information from DOE or Department of War reactor demonstration programs in support of commercial reactor license applications. That looks like exactly the regulatory path Oklo is counting on for its stated plan to move Groves-2 from DOE-authorized pilot operation to NRC-licensed commercial operation: data earned under DOE authorization counting toward the commercial application. It makes the two-track story more credible — but it is not itself a license, and it produces no public docket or comment period until Oklo actually files.

3. TCEQ and DSHS — nothing on Groves-2 yet. We searched every publicly queryable TCEQ record set: water-quality permits (active and pending, via data.texas.gov), the pending NSR and Title V air-permit lists, pending radioactive-material license applications, and the hazardous-waste registration and annual-report datasets. Zero results for Oklo, Atomic Alchemy, Mineral Springs Frontier, Proto Town, or Eiden. TDLR TABS shows exactly one registration for the site — the Groves-1 building above. No “Groves-2” appears in any of them. Two caveats: TCEQ moved new pending air-permit applications on August 31, 2026 to a JavaScript-only Permit Search Portal that can’t be searched programmatically, and the Commissioners’ Integrated Database was returning server errors during our check. (Also, for anyone who wondered: the “09.08.26 TCEQ notice” posted on Caldwell County’s site is a septic-system permit amendment for Blue Sky Lockhart LP, WQ0016177001 — not Oklo.) One correction to our own scope, though: for a hot-cell processing facility, the primary state licensing authority over possession, use, and storage of radioactive material in Texas is not TCEQ but the Department of State Health Services — Texas is an Agreement State, and DSHS’s Radiation Control Program regulates byproduct material of exactly the kind a Lu-177 plant would handle. TCEQ’s jurisdiction is mostly downstream, at the disposal end. We’ll be watching DSHS’s license roster alongside TCEQ’s databases.

4. What Oklo’s own deck reveals. The teach-in presentation shows the commercial isotope production reactor — “compact,” “multiple reactors per facility,” 100% capacity allocated to isotope production — still marked “WORKING DRAFT.” It also names the isotope pipeline beyond Lu-177: Tb-161 (from Gd-160 targets), Pb-212 via a thorium generator (“Oklo route”), and I-131. Each of those is a different target chemistry and a different waste profile.

How Lu-177 is actually made — and what that produces

Oklo has already disclosed its target feedstock: in September it announced receiving two grams of enriched ytterbium-176 from Actinide Inc., “a stable isotope that with the addition of a single neutron in a nuclear reactor yields Lu-177.” That fixes the production route — and it’s the one the trade press sometimes gets wrong.

There are two reactor routes:

Route Reaction Product
Direct ¹⁷⁶Lu(n,γ)¹⁷⁷Lu Carrier-added Lu-177 — mixed with stable lutetium
Indirect ¹⁷⁶Yb(n,γ)¹⁷⁷Yb → β⁻ → ¹⁷⁷Lu No-carrier-added Lu-177 — the form preferred for drug labeling

Oklo’s Yb-176 feedstock means the indirect route: neutrons turn Yb-176 into Yb-177 (half-life 1.9 hours), which beta-decays into Lu-177 (half-life 6.65 days). Chemists then separate the lutetium from the bulk ytterbium. The “no-carrier-added” quality is what makes it useful for radiopharmaceuticals — every atom of lutetium in the vial is radioactive, so more of the targeting molecule carries a payload.

What this chemistry means for a site:

  • The bulk material is stable ytterbium, not radioactive waste — but it’s valuable, and it’s radioactive. Recovering and recycling the enriched Yb-176 is a core part of the process (Yb-176 is expensive and globally scarce — DeWitte’s blog post jokes that every isotope company “has access to 200 grams of ytterbium” from the same Russian stockpile). Expect on-site inventory of enriched, irradiated target material.

  • Ytterbium and lutetium are neighboring lanthanides — chemically almost twins. Separating them takes repeated extraction steps, which means a hot cell running extraction chemistry: acids, resins, solvent, filters, and gloves. That process waste — solid and liquid — is low-level radioactive waste, and it’s the ordinary daily output of any Lu-177 plant. Groves-1, running 100 watts for 100 hours, was certified as a Very Small Quantity Generator of hazardous waste under RCRA/TCEQ with “very small quantities” of radioactive waste (details). A processing facility is a different animal. What will Groves-2’s waste generator classification be? Where do the liquid and solid waste streams go? Texas’s rules give a concrete menu of answers, and we’ve pulled TCEQ’s own RG-486 guide on radioactive material exemptions into the record:

  • Waste that meets the rule’s criteria is “exempt by rule” from radioactive-waste disposal requirements and can go to an ordinary or hazardous-waste landfill instead of a licensed low-level waste facility. Disposal facilities routinely require a TCEQ “exemption concurrence” letter before accepting such a shipment; requests are free, go to TCEQ’s Radioactive Materials Division, and typically take about two weeks. That’s a concrete, checkable paper trail to look for later.

  • The “300-Day Rule” (30 TAC 336.225(c)) lets a licensed generator — with agency approval — dispose of licensed short-lived isotopes (half-lives of 300 days or less, as listed in 30 TAC 336.365 Appendix H) at a Type I municipal solid waste facility or a hazardous-waste facility. Lu-177’s 6.65-day half-life puts it squarely in that class.

  • Texas prohibits dilution to lower a waste’s classification (30 TAC 336.229), and containers can’t be averaged together to pass the limits — each container is characterized on its own. So “just dilute it” is not an option, but every decayed process stream still gets classified individually.

Read together: the ordinary, short-lived output of a Lu-177 plant — resins, filters, gloves, decayed product — could plausibly end up at a regional landfill with an exemption concurrence in the file, never touching a licensed low-level waste facility. That is normal, regulated practice, not a red flag — but it means the endpoint of this facility’s everyday waste could be a local solid-waste facility, and the people living around it would want to know. Which facility? Which waste streams? Oklo has not said.

  • The product itself is a high-activity beta emitter. Lu-177 emits betas up to 497 keV (mean ~134 keV) and low-energy gammas at 113 keV (10.4%) and 208 keV (6.4%). Shielding a vial is easy; shielding a pilot production line processing grams of material at clinical strength is an engineering question the public has not yet seen answered. And with a 6.65-day half-life, product (and waste) decays on a timescale that forces continuous make-and-ship operations.
  • The reactor’s coolant will be activated. Any pool-type reactor produces trace activation in its water, including tritium. The Groves-1 determination waved this away as negligible — reasonable for 100 watts and a hundred hours, not extrapolatable to a production reactor whose power level has not been disclosed. The commercial reactor in Oklo’s deck is a “working draft.” What is the thermal power of the Groves-2 pilot reactor? That single number drives the decay heat, the cooling requirements, the fission-product inventory in the fuel, and the size of the emergency planning zone. Oklo has not said — but the chemistry gives us a way to estimate it.
  • Spent fuel. Groves-1’s five GAIA assemblies were designed never to be refueled (companion piece). Groves-2 will be refueled, on a schedule Oklo hasn’t published. The Groves-1 NEPA describes interim storage in the reactor tank before shipment to licensed facilities or “Oklo’s planned fuel recycling facility.” Where does Groves-2’s spent fuel sit, for how long, and under whose oversight? (Oklo also just signed “multiple definitive, multiyear contracts” with Centrus for HALEU; per Nuclear News, Oklo will make prepayments to Centrus to support building new enrichment capacity at the American Centrifuge plant in Ohio, formalizing the June letter of intent. Deliveries begin 2029, and the fuel is earmarked for Oklo’s planned 1.2-GW Ohio power plant, the refueling of Aurora-INL, and “other future Aurora deployments.” Is any HALEU ever headed to Texas? The releases say powerhouses only. Ask them.)

What reactor does this chemistry actually demand?

Oklo has not disclosed the Forge reactor’s power. But the Lu-177 math is public, and every facility in the world that actually runs it is on the record.

The production rate scales directly with neutron flux. The Yb-176(n,γ) reaction has a small thermal capture cross section — about 3.1 barns — so the only way to make useful quantities is to push a very large number of neutrons through the target. The result, per a 2021 review in the European Journal of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging: “Current production of ¹⁷⁷Lu, both for carrier added and no carrier added ¹⁷⁷Lu, is done in reactors with a high neutron flux” — BR2 (Belgium), FRM-II (Germany), HFR (Netherlands), IVV-2M (Russia), LVR-15 (Czech Republic), Maria (Poland), MURR (USA), OPAL (Australia), Safari (South Africa), and SM-3 (Russia) — facilities that, the review notes, “have been designed specifically to generate high [fluxes].” Of the three whose specifications are public, all are pool-type research reactors in the ten-to-twenty-megawatt class: MURR in Missouri, the only reported end-to-end domestic NCA Lu-177 producer, runs a 10 MW core; FRM-II in Garching, Germany runs 20 MW; and OPAL in Australia is a 20 MW multi-purpose reactor.

The best-documented case of Oklo’s exact route is FRM-II. A Technische Universität München radiochemistry group ran the Yb-176 indirect route there in cooperation with the commercial isotope producer ITG: at a thermal flux of ~1.3×10¹⁴ n/cm²/s, a 100 mg target of 99.7%-enriched Yb-176 yields up to ~94 GBq of NCA Lu-177 over a 14-day irradiation, and targets up to 150 mg produced up to 120 GBq (~3.2 Ci) per cycle. For scale, a typical patient dose of Lu-177 is 7–9 GBq: one two-week cycle of a 20 MW research reactor makes on the order of a few hundred patient doses. That is the actual tempo of this chemistry.

Now run the numbers against what Oklo has disclosed:

  • Two grams of feedstock. Oklo’s entire disclosed Yb-176 inventory is two grams. At FRM-II-like yields, that works out to roughly 1.5 TBq (~40 Ci) per cycle — a few hundred patient doses. Commercial NCA Lu-177 production runs on kilograms of enriched Yb-176 per year. Two grams is a pilot quantity.
  • 100 watts vs. 10–20 megawatts. Groves-1 is capped at 100 watts by its own DOE determination. Neutron flux scales roughly with reactor power: FRM-II’s 20 MW produces ~10¹⁴ n/cm²/s, so scaling down 200,000-fold, 100 watts lands near ~10⁹ n/cm²/s — roughly five orders of magnitude below the flux band where this chemistry produces anything useful. (Even the simpler carrier-added route, which skips the Yb/Lu separation, is “typically produced at nuclear reactors with intermediate neutron flux of 1–3×10¹⁴ n/cm²/s,” per the FRM-II documentation.) No watt-scale reactor gets there.

So the inference — from the chemistry and the public record, not from anything Oklo has said — is that if Groves-2 Forge is what Oklo describes, it contains a reactor at least two to five orders of magnitude larger than Groves-1: a megawatt-class, high-flux machine, more like a small research reactor than a scaled-up test reactor. The public has not seen that design. The pilot reactor’s power, fuel, and cooling have not been published, and the commercial reactor in Oklo’s own deck is still a “working draft.” That is precisely what the pending DOE NEPA determination for Groves-2 should say — and if it describes something far smaller, the question will be how much Lu-177 it can actually make.

The business reality check

The business picture, for what it’s worth, cuts both ways: Oklo is also signing long-dated hardware commitments, like its “strategic manufacturing agreement” with U.S. Forged Rings covering 27 Aurora module-equivalents reserved across 2030–2035 (first deliveries 2030). That’s a decade-long roadmap written in contracts, on the same equity-financed foundation as everything else. The press release names no buyer, no price, no production volume, and no federal funding (The Inference). The company’s own guidance, as covered by DiscoveryAlert and in its Q3 2026 results, points the first isotope revenue to early 2027 — from the NRC-licensed Idaho laboratory, not from Texas. Groves-1 cost roughly $65M plus ~$10M in fuel, per management on the Q3 call.

The demand story is real: Novartis’s Lu-177 drugs sold roughly $2.8B combined in 2025, with Pluvicto growing 43% in Q2. But “shortage” does not mean “empty field” — the DiscoveryAlert piece’s supplier table (MURR, SHINE, ITM/Eckert & Ziegler, and others) shows a crowded race to fill it, and the University of Missouri Research Reactor remains the only reported end-to-end domestic NCA producer. Meanwhile Oklo reported a $123.3M net loss for the first nine months of 2026, funded by $2.3B in at-the-market stock sales — the same equity that built Groves-1.

And note the scale gap we quantified above: Oklo’s entire disclosed Yb-176 inventory so far is two grams — less than a penny. It also tells you this supply chain is at an early stage, with domestic enrichment (Actinide’s calutrons) not yet proven at scale.

What we’re watching

  1. Site selection for Groves-2 — any new TABS entry at Proto-Town (or elsewhere), and any announcement. Oklo says it plans to begin construction within 12 months, so the window is now.
  2. The DOE NEPA determination for Groves-2 — it will be the first public statement of the pilot reactor’s power, fuel, cooling, accident analysis, and waste. Based on the Lu-177 math above, we expect it to describe a machine far larger than the 100-watt Groves-1; if it doesn’t, the question is how much isotope it can actually make.
  3. NRC filings when the commercial track begins — a Part 52 license and materials licensing, with the usual public comment period. Expect Oklo to invoke the new October rule to fold in its DOE pilot-program data.
  4. TCEQ permits for any change in waste generator status or process discharges — check TCEQ’s new Permit Search Portal monthly, since it’s the only place post–August 31 applications appear, and the CID for anything pending before the Commission. Also watch DSHS licensing for the processing facility, and TCEQ exemption concurrences once waste characterization begins.
  5. Status of our FOIA requests to the NRC and DOE — still pending (tracked in our Groves-1 piece), and still the cleanest path to the reactor design documents Oklo cites as trade secrets.

Groves-2 Forge is a different category of facility from the 100-watt experiment its name borrows from: a reactor plus a chemical plant, producing material potent enough to treat cancer — and, as the chemistry suggests, almost certainly a megawatt-class machine — on a site whose location, power level, and safety basis the public has not yet seen. The name says “Groves.” The record does not say Lockhart. Until it does — or until it says somewhere else — the questions stand.
