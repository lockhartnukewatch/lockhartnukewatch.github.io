How an Isotope Production Facility Should Run

There’s a lot here about problems with Oklo and their Groves project. Why? It’s a noble pursuit, to try and produce rare and hard-to-obtain, life-saving medicine. I had a friend pass away from cancer. Maybe medicine like this could have helped? He did receive radiotherapy at one point.

Our criticism comes from a lack of transparency and community participation in the site selection, the approval and the build-out. It hasn’t been a two-way dialogue. It’s been, this is what’s coming, it’s inevitable.

It doesn’t have to be like this! There is a way to produce radiological medicine (as Oklo advertises) in a such a way that builds trust through transparency, partners with affected communities, submits to public regulatory review, and even reduces the world’s nuclear weapon arsenal.

What we should have instead

TMC Group: a private producer publishing real numbers

The source behind one of the more concrete production disclosures in the Lu-177 story is TMC Group, the North Vancouver-based manufacturer of high-purity enriched stable isotopes.

TMC manufactures Ytterbium-176, Gallium-69, and Gadolinium-160 for medical radioisotope production, says it has been doing so since 2004, and has sold and shipped enriched stable isotopes globally since 2004 (its own cumulative figure runs past 13 kg shipped as of mid-2026).

In May 2026, TMC announced new production lines adding 50% capacity to support next-generation radiotheranostics. Earlier reporting on TMC’s numbers – later mapped by ProGen Search in The Lu-177 Capacity Number Nobody Publishes – put TMC’s 2025 output at more than two kilograms of elemental Yb-176, at isotopic purity above 99.7%. By October 2025, TMC had also reported more than 250 g of Yb-176 in stock and available for purchase.

In August 2025, TMC Group joined the Canadian Nuclear Isotope Council (CNIC), a not-for-profit network of Canadian isotope stakeholders – an angle that makes TMC a reasonable example of a private isotope producer engaging a broader community/industry coalition while publishing production figures of its own.

Even a private producer like TMC is subject to Canada’s nuclear regulatory framework, and its operations run under that oversight rather than outside it. And on the community front, how TMC works with the communities in which it operates is a two-way conversation that deserves the same public scrutiny as its production numbers.

Bruce Power ran its Lu-177 production under a public regulator

Bruce Power is the first commercial nuclear power reactor in the world to produce lutetium-177, using a first-of-a-kind Isotope Production System (IPS) – neutron capture of Yb-176 in a CANDU reactor, then hot-cell separation into Lu-177.

CANDU reactors in and of themselves are super-interesting! They were designed during the cold war to promote peaceful nuclear energy, in such a way that reduced, rather than increased weapons-grade nuclear material. With such a versatile fuel cycle, a CANDU can draw down, rather than proliferate elements that build bombs.

Irradiation began in 2022; a second Unit 7 line came online in 2024; an on-site hot cell completed construction in July 2026 and is moving through licensing and commissioning, with target-carrier removal as its first stated role.

On Aug. 17, 2026, the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission amended the licensing basis for Lu-177 production at Bruce A and B. On Aug. 18, the CNSC approved the on-site hot cell facility.

Bruce’s isotope production has run under a public regulator with public licensing-basis documents and a public hearing record behind it. The revenue-sharing agreement Bruce Power struck with First Nations – as reported by the National Observer – is a welcome acknowledgment of Indigenous partnership, even as questions of consent and benefit-sharing remain.

The technical ambition Oklo has sketched for Groves-2 Forge – high-flux reactor plus hot cell plus Yb-176 to Lu-177 – has been licensed in Canada’s framework. Oklo’s version remains a DOE pilot whose reactor deck is a “WORKING DRAFT.”

Beyond Bruce, another public-pathway example of isotope production: OPG’s Laurentis Energy Partners has cleared to produce more life-saving isotopes at the Darlington nuclear plant.

MURR: a US reactor producing Lu-177 end to end

The University of Missouri Research Reactor (MURR) is the only reported end-to-end domestic producer of no-carrier-added Lu-177 in the United States, and it produced GMP Lu-177 for global distribution – 52 weeks a year, per ProGen Search’s isotope production map.

It is a useful counter-example because it shows isotope production operating inside a public, government-run research reactor framework rather than an opaque commercial startup – a different path to the same product as Oklo’s Groves-2 Forge.

ISIS: a UK accelerator-based isotope facility

The UK’s ISIS Neutron and Muon Source operates an 800 MeV proton accelerator producing intense neutron and muon beams for science and medicine – a public, national facility rather than an opaque commercial startup.

It is a useful counter-example because it shows accelerator-based isotope production running in the open, in publicly owned hands, rather than through a secretive commercial venture.

ProGen Search built a transparency methodology for the isotope supply chain

ProGen Search’s Isotope Production Map is a transparency-first attempt to answer “who produces what, and how much actually came out?” Its capacity tiers (announced, commissioned, GMP or national approval, actual output), source register, and evidence-class system exist precisely because – in ProGen’s words – “your supplier says they’ve got capacity. Have they ever actually made any? Most of the time you can’t find out. Not from public material, not from the data room, not from the trade press. Which is exactly the gap that this closes.”

In The Lu-177 Capacity Number Nobody Publishes, ProGen maps six public statements on Lu-177 and Yb-176 capacity and classifies each as nameplate, company-reported output, forward guidance, or contractual share. A lot has to happen between grams of feedstock, neutron bombardment, medicine refinement and patient distribution to deliver actual life-saving value.

Compare and contrast with Oklo Groves

  • Private, disclosed production vs. opaque corporate opacity. TMC Group publishes production figures of its own accord (more than 2 kg of elemental Yb-176 in 2025; more than 250 g in stock; more than 13 kg shipped cumulatively since 2004) and joined the Canadian Nuclear Isotope Council, a not-for-profit isotope coalition, in August 2025. Oklo’s own feedstock story runs through Actinide, whose calutron/HALEU claims have met opacity from the other direction – NRC records silent, company website omitting the uranium, no production mass disclosed.
  • Licensed, public oversight vs. withheld safety basis. Bruce Power’s Lu-177 production sits under a public regulator with public licensing basis and public hearings. Oklo’s Groves sits under DOE authorization, with DOE safety design agreement and safety reviews withheld from public review and comment.
  • Real production vs. pilot disclosure. Bruce’s IPS has produced Lu-177 commercially since 2022, with a hot cell approved for operation in August 2026. Oklo’s Groves-1 is a 100-watt experiment, cleared for unrestricted operations in November 2026. Groves-2 Forge and it’s production scale supplier still remain undisclosed.

What an isotope production facility should do

The positive counterexamples suggest a short, checkable list:

  1. Publish design documents; through open sources where possible.
  2. Publish safety analyses and DOE/NRC reviews; accept public comment.
  3. Run under a public regulator with public licensing basis and hearings.
  4. Classify supply-chain claims into evidence tiers; publish source registers; record unknowns as unknowns.
  5. Put community engagement, emergency planning, and first-responder planning in the public realm from day one.

What we’re watching

  1. The DOE NEPA determination for Groves-2 Forge, and Bruce Power’s hot cell licensing and commissioning as the transparency benchmark.
  2. The status of our FOIA requests to the DOE – still the cleanest path to Groves design documents.
  3. Whether the Isotope Supply Chain findings hold up as the Lu-177 market develops.
